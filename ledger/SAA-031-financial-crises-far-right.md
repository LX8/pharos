## Claim
The descriptive regularity is ROBUST: across ~140 years, 20 advanced economies and 800+ elections, far-right vote share rises ~30% on average in the ~5 years after systemic banking crises, alongside shrinking government majorities and rising polarization/fragmentation (Funke, Schularick & Trebesch 2016, EER). Two layers are weaker. The scope condition "specific to financial crises, not ordinary recessions" holds descriptively in their own data but its causal/uniqueness reading is CONTESTED (Margalit 2019 argues economic insecurity is a marginal, not primary, driver of populism; cultural factors dominate). Whether the regularity is weakening in recent data is OPEN — average effects fade after ~5 years, yet post-2008 far-right gains persisted unusually long.

## Evidence
For: FST (2016) document a robust ~30% far-right vote-share rise after financial crises over 1870-2014, 20 countries, 800+ elections, with no comparable dynamic after normal recessions or non-financial macro shocks; corroborated descriptively by Mian-Sufi-Trebbi on post-crisis polarization/fractionalization, and causally at the micro level by Gyöngyösi & Verner (2022, J. Finance), who use Hungarian foreign-currency-debt exposure as quasi-random variation and find a large, persistent rise in far-right support. Against: Margalit (2019, JEP) shows the share of populist support explained by economic insecurity is modest and argues financial shocks are more trigger than root cause, with cultural/immigration attitudes only marginally economic — challenging the "financial crises uniquely drive this" framing rather than the raw correlation. The far-left shows no comparable gain, and effects are estimated to taper after ~5 years.

## Why it matters
If financial crises systematically and distinctively boost the far right and fragment legislatures, then financial-stability policy and post-crisis governance are first-order tools for containing authoritarian-leaning movements — and the window of elevated risk is predictable (roughly the five years after a banking crisis), making early-warning and pre-emptive democratic safeguards actionable.
