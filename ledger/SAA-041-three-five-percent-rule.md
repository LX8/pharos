## Claim

The 3.5% rule as popularly stated — *no government has ever withstood a challenge in which
3.5% of its population mobilized at a peak event* — is **no longer true, by its own author's
account**. Chenoweth's 2020 cautionary paper (the source she designates as the canonical
citation for the rule) documents two above-threshold failures in the expanded NAVCO 1.2 data
(1945–2014): **Brunei 1962** — an armed revolt of ~4,000 participants, about 4% of a total
population under 90,000, crushed in ten days after British reinforcement of the sultan — and
**Bahrain 2011–2014** — a nonviolent uprising against King Hamad with purportedly 100,000
participants at peak, over 6% of the population, which decisively failed amid Saudi-backed
security support. Her verdict: the rule is a tendency, not a law.

What survives falsification is a **strong descriptive association**: of the 18 maximalist
campaigns reaching ≥3.5% peak participation, 88.89% (16 of 18) succeeded, against 60.98% in
the 1–3.5% band and progressively less below (table significance p < .003). Only ~4.6% of
the 389 maximalist campaigns of 1945–2013 ever crossed the threshold at all.

## Evidence

**FOR (the tendency layer).** The NAVCO 1.2 participation-success table is monotone and
significant: ≥3.5% → 88.89% success (n=18); 1–3.5% → 60.98% (n=41); 0.25–1% → 45.65% (n=92);
under 0.0035% → 4.17% (n=24). Large peak participation is among the strongest observed
correlates of maximalist-campaign success.

**AGAINST (the law/prescription layer).** (a) The two documented exceptions above.
(b) The metric counts *peak* participation only, not cumulative participation or momentum —
and Chenoweth & Belgioioso (2019) find that momentum (participation mass × event velocity)
predicts irregular leader exit "regardless of whether the mobilization hit the 3.5%
threshold". (c) The inverse direction is decisive against prescriptive use: **among all
successful maximalist nonviolent campaigns, 83% succeeded without ever crossing 3.5%** —
the threshold is neither necessary nor sufficient. (d) The rule derives exclusively from
*maximalist* campaigns (removing an incumbent or winning territorial independence); there is
no evidence it transfers to reformist campaigns — climate, rights, policy change — yet
movements such as Extinction Rebellion explicitly built mobilization strategy on that
transfer. (e) The success rate of nonviolent campaigns has itself declined since the 2010s
(see [`SAA-009`](SAA-009-no-universal-threshold.md)), so a threshold estimated on 1945–2014
outcomes may overstate present-day odds.

## Why it matters

This is arguably the single most-cited number in strategy against authoritarian regimes, and
the gap between what it says (a descriptive statistic over 18 historical cases) and how it is
used (a mobilization target that guarantees victory) is exactly the kind of overclaim this
ledger exists to catch. Chenoweth's own warning is the strategic content: chasing a
participation benchmark without building broad public constituency, organization, and
defection pathways "may create a loud but wildly unpopular minority". The entry is also a
model case for the ledger's method — the correction comes from the original researcher
stress-testing her own headline finding in public, which is the behavior a falsification
culture is supposed to reward, not punish. Companion entries:

## Honest limits

- **Small n at the top band.** 18 campaigns ≥3.5%; a single additional failure moves 88.89%
  to 83.3%. The p < .003 significance attaches to the participation-success association
  across all bands, not to the 3.5% cut-point specifically — the cut is a communicative
  convenience, not an estimated breakpoint.
- **Measurement is journalistic.** Peak participation is compiled largely from news reports
  (conservative estimate chosen when sources vary), with population denominators from Penn
  World Tables / CNTS / archival records. Both exceptions and non-exceptions inherit that
  noise.
- **Campaign-type nuance.** Brunei 1962 was an *armed* campaign; the strictly nonviolent
  form of the rule has only Bahrain as its documented exception. Popular statements of the
  rule rarely draw this distinction, so the entry grades the claim as commonly used.
- **A boundary case is visible in the primary source.** The paper's campaign chart displays
  the failed Djibouti Arab Spring at a rounded "3.5" — the 16-of-18 table arithmetic implies
  it falls just below the threshold, but it illustrates how sensitive the exception count is
  to rounding and estimation at the boundary.
- **Both exceptions share one profile** — small monarchies with access to overwhelming
  foreign military reinforcement — and Chenoweth notes the rule "appears to persist" outside
  that profile. That is a hypothesis about scope conditions, not yet an independently tested
  finding.
