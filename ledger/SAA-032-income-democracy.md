## Claim
The cross-national CORRELATION between income/development and democracy (Lipset 1959) is ROBUST: it has been replicated for decades and is broadly accepted. The CAUSAL layer — that rising income within a country causes democratization — is CONTESTED. Acemoglu, Johnson, Robinson & Yared (2008) show the association largely vanishes once country fixed effects (long-run divergent development paths set at historical critical junctures) are added, and their IV estimates find no short/medium-run causal effect. But that null is itself disputed: Benhabib, Corvalan & Spiegel (2013) show the positive income-democracy link survives country fixed effects once the censoring/nonlinearity of bounded democracy indices is modeled, and Treisman (2015) finds a real medium-run (10-20 yr) effect conditional on leader turnover. So: correlation layer ROBUST, causal layer CONTESTED (genuine live debate, not a settled refutation in either direction).

## Evidence
FOR a causal income effect: the raw cross-national correlation is large and durable (Lipset 1959 and the huge subsequent literature); Benhabib/Corvalan/Spiegel (2013, Economics Letters) show the positive relation is robust to country fixed effects once the censoring of democracy indices (mass at boundaries) is handled — i.e. AJRY's null is plausibly a linear-model artifact on a bounded dependent variable; Treisman (2015, AJPS) finds development raises democracy over a 10-20 year horizon contingent on leader turnover. AGAINST: AJRY (2008, AER) show that within-country, including fixed effects and time effects, the income-democracy coefficient is small and statistically insignificant, and IV (savings rate, trade-weighted income) estimates are also null; they argue the cross-sectional correlation reflects deep historical paths, not a causal income channel. The decisive point is that the within-country causal coefficient flips sign/significance depending on estimator (linear vs censored/nonlinear), sample (colonies vs non-colonies), and time horizon — the hallmark of a genuinely contested causal claim, while the correlation itself is not in dispute.

## Why it matters
Modernization theory underwrites a common optimistic prior — that economic growth automatically erodes autocracy — used to justify "engagement"/trade-with-dictatorships policy and to predict that rising-income authoritarian states (e.g. China) will liberalize. If the causal effect is weak, conditional, or absent within countries (AJRY), growth alone is not a reliable democratizing force and may even entrench incumbents in the short run (Treisman). Separating the robust correlation from the contested causation prevents the headline "wealth brings democracy" from being treated as a law when the within-country evidence is unsettled.
