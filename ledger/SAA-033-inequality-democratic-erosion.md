## Claim
The claim grades by layer. The ASSOCIATIONAL core is ROBUST: across modern cross-national data, higher economic inequality is one of the strongest and most replicable predictors of where and when democracies erode, and inequality robustly harms democratic consolidation. But the CAUSAL and SCOPE layers are genuinely CONTESTED: inequality and political institutions are mutually endogenous (the reverse arrow — democracy reducing inequality — is itself not robust), the sign and shape of the inequality–regime relationship is disputed across canonical theories, and the inequality→populism / inequality→instability channels are conditional and confounded by cultural and group-based (horizontal) factors. Defaulting to CONTESTED because the headline "inequality causes erosion" overstates what the design-strongest evidence can carry, even though the correlation is solid.

## Evidence
FOR: Rau & Stokes (PNAS 2025), on 92 countries 1995–2020 (23 erosion episodes), find inequality a robust predictor of erosion across 100+ models and multiple inequality measures (post-tax Gini, top-1/10%/bottom-50% shares), with national wealth offering only weaker insulation; they argue partisan polarization is a key mediator and that reverse causation is unlikely in their temporal models. Houle (World Politics 2009) independently finds inequality robustly harms democratic CONSOLIDATION. AGAINST/COMPLICATION: Houle finds inequality does NOT affect democratization (no net effect) — the relationship is conditional on which transition is at stake. Canonical theories disagree on sign/shape: Boix (2003) predicts equality promotes democracy (monotonic), Acemoglu–Robinson predict an inverted-U (middling inequality most destabilizing), and Ansell & Samuels (2010/2014) argue INCOME inequality can PROMOTE democratization (opposite sign) once land inequality is separated out. Acemoglu, Naidu, Restrepo & Robinson (NBER 19746, 2013/2015) find democracy's effect on inequality is NOT robust — confirming the institutions↔inequality endogeneity that undercuts clean causal identification. On the populism channel, the economics-vs-culture debate is unsettled (observational designs favor economics, survey experiments favor culture), and the inequality–conflict nexus is conditional (Bartusevičius 2014; class inequality breeds coups more than civil wars per Houle 2016).

## Why it matters
If the robust associational core were mistaken for a settled causal law, anti-backsliding strategy would over-index on redistribution as a democracy-saving lever — yet the evidence that institutions move inequality (the reverse arrow) is itself weak, and the populism channel is heavily mediated by cultural/status grievances. Correctly separating the robust "inequality is a strong risk marker" finding from the contested "inequality causes erosion, and reducing it will reliably protect democracy" inference is what keeps the ledger honest and policy-useful. See [`SAA-006`](SAA-006-status-threat-vs-economics.md) for the adjacent (still OPEN) individual-level status-threat-vs-economic-anxiety debate — this entry concerns *macro* structural inequality and regime-level erosion, a distinct unit of analysis, not double-counted.
