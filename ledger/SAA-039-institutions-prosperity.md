## Claim
The cross-national CORRELATION between measured institutional quality (protection against expropriation, constraints on the executive, rule of law) and long-run prosperity is ROBUST: large, durable across datasets and decades, and accepted even by the sharpest critics cited here — Glaeser, La Porta, Lopez-de-Silanes & Shleifer (2004) grant the association while attacking its interpretation. The CAUSAL layer — Acemoglu, Johnson & Robinson's claim that institutions are the fundamental determinant of long-run income, identified by using European settler mortality as an instrument for *current* institutions (protection against expropriation risk, 1985–95), which the theory holds persisted from colonial-era arrangements (AJR 2001), reinforced by the post-1500 "reversal of fortune" (AJR 2002) and popularized as the inclusive-vs-extractive vocabulary in *Why Nations Fail* (Acemoglu & Robinson 2012) — is CONTESTED. The identification is challenged on the data (Albouy 2012: the settler-mortality series is partly assigned from other countries and heterogeneous populations; under his corrections the IV loses robustness), on the exclusion restriction and construct (Glaeser et al. 2004: the institution measures are ex-post outcome ratings, and the instruments predict human capital as well as institutions), and on the reversal evidence itself (Chanda, Cook & Putterman 2014: tracking peoples rather than places, fortune persisted — no reversal). AJR's published Reply (2012) defends the original results as robust. The 2024 Sveriges Riksbank Prize recognizes the program's influence, not additional measurement, and under this ledger's rules does not raise the grade. Net: correlation ROBUST, causal/primacy layer CONTESTED — a genuine live debate, not a settled refutation in either direction. The single grade tracks the contested causal headline.

## Evidence
FOR the causal claim: AJR (2001, AER) instrument current institutions with European settler mortality and estimate large effects of protection against expropriation on modern income per capita, arguing mortality shaped whether colonizers built settler ("neo-European") or extractive institutions that then persisted. AJR (2002, QJE) document the reversal of fortune — among colonized territories, those relatively rich in 1500 (by urbanization/population density) are relatively poor today — which weighs against pure-geography explanations. Rodrik, Subramanian & Trebbi (2004, JEG), using the AJR settler-mortality instrument among others, find institutional quality "trumps" geography and trade integration in explaining income levels (a related design that reuses the same instrument, not an independent one). AJR's Reply (2012, AER) argues Albouy's corrections discard much of the sample despite available mortality evidence and that outlier-robust estimates survive. AGAINST / caveats: Albouy (2012, AER) shows 36 of 64 sample countries are assigned mortality rates from other countries, often from incomparable populations (laborers, bishops, soldiers on campaign) combined in ways favoring the hypothesis; controlling for these issues, the mortality–expropriation first stage weakens and the IV estimates become unreliable (often with very wide confidence intervals). Glaeser et al. (2004, JEG) argue the institutional measures are subjective ex-post assessments rather than durable rules; that settler mortality/density predict human capital as well as institutions, straining the exclusion restriction; and that human capital may be the more basic source of growth, with institutions improving afterward. Chanda, Cook & Putterman (2014, AEJ: Macro) find that once populations' places of origin are accounted for, the post-Columbian reversal disappears — fortune persisted for peoples — supporting an embodied-characteristics channel over place-based institutions. The decisive point: the headline causal estimates flip in robustness with defensible data-construction and specification choices, and a credible rival mechanism (human capital) fits much of the same evidence — the hallmark of a genuinely contested causal claim atop an undisputed correlation.

## Why it matters
The AJR program is the canonical scholarly backbone for the claim that guardrail institutions — rule of law, constraints on executives, broadly shared political and economic rights — carry long-run material payoffs, which is the wager behind treating institutional erosion as an early-warning signal. If the causal layer were settled, "protect inclusive institutions" would be an empirically grounded prosperity policy, not only a normative one; because it is contested, the ledger must not launder the robust correlation (or a Nobel) into the law "inclusive institutions cause prosperity." The layered grade keeps both halves honest: institutional quality and prosperity travel together reliably (so institutional decline co-occurring with economic decline remains a meaningful monitored signal), while the direction and primacy of causation stay open. Read with SAA-032 (income→democratization contested in the other direction, by an overlapping author team): neither arrow between wealth and institutions is settled — a caution against both "growth will save democracy" and "strong institutions guarantee prosperity" as mechanical predictions.
