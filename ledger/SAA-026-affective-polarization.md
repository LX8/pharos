## Claim
The grade splits by layer. Descriptive/measurement layer is ROBUST: in the US, partisans' growing dislike and distrust of the out-party — distinct from ideological distance — is reliably measured (ANES feeling-thermometer in-party minus out-party difference scores) and has risen substantially since the 1980s. The cross-national generalization is weaker: the rise is US-distinctive, not universal — several OECD democracies show flat or declining affective polarization. The causal layer — that affective polarization itself degrades support for democracy / democratic norms — is genuinely CONTESTED, and the overall entry takes the more conservative CONTESTED grade because the headline that motivates this ledger is the causal one.

## Evidence
FOR the descriptive core: Iyengar et al. (2019, Annu. Rev. Polit. Sci.) synthesize a large literature; the in-party/out-party thermometer gap is a stable, validated measure (high test-retest reliability) and the rise survives correction for ANES survey-mode artifacts. Mason (2018, Uncivil Agreement) grounds it in social-identity sorting. AGAINST overreach: (1) Cross-nationally, Boxell, Gentzkow & Shapiro (REStat 2024) find the US had the LARGEST increase among OECD countries studied while several countries DECREASED — so "rising" is US-distinctive, not a democratic-world law. (2) On causation, Broockman, Kalla & Westwood (AJPS 2023, "Maybe Not") run five experiments manipulating affective polarization and find little downstream effect on democratic-norm or accountability outcomes; Voelkel et al.'s 32,059-person megastudy (Science 2024) finds the treatments that most reduce partisan animosity are largely NOT the ones that reduce antidemocratic attitudes — the two outcomes dissociate. Druckman et al. separately show much measured animus rests on misperception of the typical out-partisan.

## Why it matters
For a ledger on containing authoritarianism, the temptation is to treat "reduce affective polarization" as a lever on democratic backsliding. The robust descriptive finding (Americans increasingly loathe the out-party) does not license the policy headline (therefore cutting that loathing protects democracy). The best current experimental evidence suggests partisan animosity and antidemocratic attitudes are partly separable problems — so depolarization interventions may not, by themselves, shore up democratic norms, and resources aimed at norm-protection should target norms directly rather than assuming the animosity channel.
