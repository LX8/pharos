## Claim
As a description of Chenoweth & Stephan's NAVCO 1.x data (323 maximalist campaigns, 1900-2006), the 53% vs 26% success gap is reported accurately and is robust at that descriptive layer. The CAUSAL interpretation — that being nonviolent makes a campaign substantially more likely to succeed — is genuinely contested: it is sensitive to case inclusion/coding and, per a 2026 replication, to model specification and to misreported significance. The overall claim is therefore graded CONTESTED, splitting ROBUST (descriptive comparison in that dataset) from CONTESTED (the causal "nonviolence works better" reading the headline implies).

## Evidence
FOR: Stephan & Chenoweth (2008, International Security 33:1) and the 2011 book report 53% nonviolent vs 26% violent success over 323 campaigns; "more than twice as effective" is the canonical, heavily-cited finding, replicated across NAVCO updates and broadly accepted as a descriptive regularity. AGAINST (causal layer): Anisin (2020, Critical Sociology) argues NAVCO omits failed nonviolent uprisings and successful violent revolutions; adding 19th-century and previously unanalyzed cases yields nonviolent ~48% vs unarmed-violence 60-61% and reactive violence 60% — flipping the raw comparison and exposing case-selection/coding sensitivity. Crabtree & Holbein (2026 working paper, "Is Peaceful Resistance Really Better?") report that three models flagged as highly significant (***) actually had p-values around 0.23, 0.32, and 0.53; a specification-curve over ~4,096 reasonable models found only ~1.8% significant (below chance); estimates go small/null under country- and campaign-fixed effects on NAVCO 2.1; and the instruments (GDP, regime type, terrain) plausibly violate the exclusion restriction. They conclude the cross-national evidence gives "no clear basis" for nonviolence being substantially more effective — though they do not claim zero advantage, and some newer work finds a modest pro-nonviolence effect for democratization.

## Why it matters
This finding is the empirical backbone of a generation of pro-democracy and anti-authoritarian strategy advice: activists, donors, and trainers cite "nonviolence works twice as well" to justify movement tactics against autocrats. If the descriptive gap is real but the causal claim is fragile, strategists should treat nonviolence as well-supported on normative and coalition-building grounds and as a strong correlate of success — but should not over-rely on a clean causal guarantee that the data cannot bear, especially given selection effects (campaigns that could go nonviolent may already be better-positioned to win).
