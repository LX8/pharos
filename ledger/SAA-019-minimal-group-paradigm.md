## Claim
The core effect is ROBUST: across many studies and a meta-analysis, mere categorization into arbitrary, conflict-free groups reliably produces in-group favoritism in reward allocation, with a small-to-moderate average effect size (meta-analytic r approximately 0.26; cooperation d approximately 0.32). The strong interpretive layer — that categorization alone is universally "sufficient" — is more qualified: an adversarial collaboration prompted by failed replications showed the discrimination effect depends on structural moderators (common vs. unilateral knowledge of group membership, simultaneous vs. sequential exchange) and salience. So grade ROBUST for the effect's existence, CONTESTED for the unconditional "sufficient under all conditions" reading.

## Evidence
FOR: Tajfel et al. (1971) found own-group favoritism in distributing real rewards where only an irrelevant classification (e.g., Klee/Kandinsky preference, or random assignment) separated groups, with no individual interest or prior hostility. Mullen, Brown & Smith (1992) meta-analyzed ~137 tests; in-group bias was significant and of moderate magnitude, moderated by salience, relevance, and status — and behavioral discrimination in minimal groups was on average at least as strong as in real groups. AGAINST: A series of close replications failed to find significant minimal-group discrimination, which is what triggered Kerr et al. (2018). That adversarial collaboration recovered the effect (d approximately 0.32) only conditionally — it was reliable under common knowledge of group membership and simultaneous exchange, weaker or absent otherwise — showing the bare "categorization is sufficient" framing omits load-bearing conditions.

## Why it matters
The minimal group finding is a cornerstone of social identity theory and a foundational empirical anchor for understanding how arbitrary "us vs. them" lines — including those manufactured by authoritarian movements with no real conflict of interest — can generate discriminatory behavior. If even meaningless categories trigger favoritism, the constructed in-groups/out-groups that authoritarian propaganda relies on are a potent and low-cost mobilization tool. The moderator nuance matters for intervention design: discrimination is amplified by mutual visibility and concurrent stakes, which points to where de-escalation leverage exists.
