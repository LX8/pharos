## Claim
McAdam, Tarrow & Tilly's "Dynamics of Contention" (2001) launched a mechanism-and-process program — explaining contentious politics via recurrent mechanisms (brokerage, diffusion, boundary/category activation, scale shift) aggregated into processes, rather than via covering laws. As a research agenda it is genuinely influential and widely taught (ROBUST as an agenda-setting intervention and as a descriptive vocabulary). But its core claim to constitute an explanatory framework is CONTESTED: critics — and the authors themselves — concede the mechanisms were catalogued "by the dozen" without precise definition, clear scope conditions, or specified test methods, making causal claims hard to falsify and prone to post-hoc identification. The grade splits by layer: the program's existence and reach is robust; its status as a rigorous, falsifiable causal theory is the disputed layer and is the basis for the overall CONTESTED grade.

## Evidence
FOR (the framework is established): The 2001 book is a heavily cited, agenda-setting work that reframed strikes, revolutions, social movements and democratization under one relational, mechanism-based lens; a decade later Mobilization ran a full symposium on it (McAdam & Tarrow 2011) and an edited volume reassessed the program (van Stekelenburg et al. 2013), evidence it became a durable research tradition rather than a one-off. AGAINST (it is too loose / post-hoc / hard to falsify): In the 2007 follow-up "Contentious Politics" and the 2011 retrospective, the authors themselves grant the original "pointed to mechanisms and processes by the dozen without defining and documenting them carefully," was unclear about "the methods and evidence" for checking explanations, and "reveled in complications." Philosopher Daniel Little, broadly sympathetic, calls the disaggregative move "a crucially important breakthrough" yet judges the framework conceptually underspecified — the MTT definition of a mechanism as "a delimited class of events that alter relations" fails to capture mechanisms as causal pathways, leaving which mechanism fires (and which outcome follows) underdetermined and dependent on factors outside the theory. That underdetermination is exactly the falsifiability complaint: the vocabulary can re-describe almost any episode after the fact.

## Why it matters
For an anti-authoritarian evidence ledger, this program is the dominant analytic toolkit for explaining how mobilization, repression, and regime contention actually unfold (e.g., scale shift in protest waves, brokerage in coalition-building, boundary activation in polarization). Knowing it is an influential but underspecified heuristic — not a predictive, falsifiable law system — sets the right epistemic expectation: it organizes comparative case analysis and identifies recurring causal moves, but it does not license confident point predictions about whether a given mobilization will scale or be repressed. Treating mechanism-talk as explanation when it is often re-description is the specific trap to avoid.
