## Claim
The association is robust: across the US (Autor, Dorn, Hanson & Majlesi 2020, AER) and 15 Western European countries (Colantone & Stanig 2018, AJPS; and their Brexit study, APSR 2018), local labor markets more exposed to the surge in Chinese imports shifted measurably toward nationalist, radical-right, protectionist and (in the US) polarized voting — and a meta-analysis of 36 quasi-experimental studies confirms a consistently positive, significant link. The causal LAYER is credible but not airtight: it rests on the now-standard shift-share/instrumented-exposure design (instrumenting EU/US imports with Chinese exports to other high-income economies), whose identifying assumptions (share- or shock-exogeneity, correlated errors) are themselves the subject of an active econometrics literature. The MAGNITUDE/scope layer is genuinely contested: the meta-analysis pooled effect falls from r=0.058 to r=0.016 after publication-bias correction, economic shocks explain only ~one-third of the populist rise, evidence concentrates in Western Europe/North America, and the economy-vs-culture mechanism is debated (they interact/mediate rather than economics being the sole driver). Net: ROBUST directional core, CONTESTED causal-magnitude headline — graded conservatively as CONTESTED.

## Evidence
FOR: ADHM (2020, AER 110(10):3139-3183) find trade-exposed US districts grew more ideologically polarized and shifted right (initially-Republican districts toward conservative Republicans), using the China-shock instrument. Colantone & Stanig (2018, AJPS 62(4):936-953) find import-exposed Western European regions increased support for nationalist/radical-right parties and shifted right, instrumenting EU imports with Chinese exports to the US. Their Brexit paper (APSR 2018) finds Leave support higher in import-shocked regions. Scheiring et al. (2024, BJPS) meta-analyze 36 studies: all find a positive economic-insecurity→populism link, significant even after correcting for publication bias; shocks explain ~1/3 of the populism rise. AGAINST: The same meta-analysis documents substantial publication bias — the standardized effect collapses from 0.058 to 0.016 after correction; right-wing populism findings (65% robust) outperform left (40%); literature is geographically narrow (W. Europe/N. America). Shift-share identification itself is contested (Goldsmith-Pinkham, Sorkin & Swift 2020; Borusyak, Hull & Jaravel 2022; Adão, Kolesár & Morales 2019): validity hinges on strong, partly untestable share/shock-exogeneity assumptions. The economic-vs-cultural-backlash debate (Norris & Inglehart) remains live; mechanisms appear to interact rather than economics dominating alone.

## Why it matters
If trade shocks causally feed far-right and protectionist politics, then trade liberalization without adequate compensation/adjustment for losers is itself a driver of democratic backsliding risk — making place-based industrial and social policy, not just rhetoric, a lever against authoritarian populism. Overstating it (economics-only, large-magnitude) misdirects that policy and ignores the cultural mediation the evidence actually shows.
